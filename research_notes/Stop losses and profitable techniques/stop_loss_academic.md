## 1. Kaminski & Lo (2014), "When do stop-loss rules stop losses?" — model, stopping premium, empirical results

### Takeaway
Under a random walk, a simple 0/1 stop-loss rule (switch from the risky asset to a safe asset after a loss threshold is breached) **always lowers expected return**. It can add value only when returns have momentum (positive serial correlation) or persistent regimes. In US data (monthly, Jan 1950 – Dec 2004, stocks with long-term government bonds as the safe asset), some stop rules added **50–100 bp per month during stop-out periods**, before trading costs.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Kaminski, K.M. & Lo, A.W., "When Do Stop-Loss Rules Stop Losses?" *Journal of Financial Markets* 18 (March 2014): 234–254. The working paper is on SSRN (abstract_id 968338), dated 2007/2008. — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp234-254.html); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=968338); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114876)
- Core theorem (abstract): "Under the Random Walk Hypothesis, simple 0/1 stop-loss rules always decrease a strategy's expected return, but in the presence of momentum, stop-loss rules can add value." — [MIT DSpace PDF (via search snippet)](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/114876/Lo_When%20Do%20Stop-Loss.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hhs/sifrwp/0063.html)
- "Stopping premium" is the paper's measure of the expected-return value a stop-loss rule adds or loses relative to buy-and-hold. The paper says it is "inextricably linked to the stochastic process driving the underlying portfolio's return". With enough positive autocorrelation the premium can be positive, because positions that move against the investor tend to keep moving that way. — [MIT DSpace (search snippet)](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/bb69ca4b-0cdc-487f-831d-63b2e84fafee)
- Empirical result (SSRN abstract): using monthly returns from January 1950 to December 2004, "certain stop-loss rules add 50 to 100 basis points per month to the buy-and-hold portfolio during stop-out periods". The figure is gross of trading frictions. — [SSRN abstract (search snippet)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=968338); [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/40384)
- Data (secondary description): the risky asset is the monthly CRSP value-weighted returns index. When stopped out, the portfolio holds long-term US government bonds (Ibbotson). — [Search summary citing SSRN/ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314895345_When_Do_Stop-Loss_Rules_Stop_Losses)
- Secondary description: the stop-loss rule reduces the portfolio's unconditional variance. Stops hurt returns in rising markets and help in falling ones. — [Search summary (quant-investing.com / BSIC)](https://bsic.it/how-to-define-a-stop-loss/) (secondary, not verified against the paper)
- A later Kaminski–Lo extension reportedly uses daily futures data from January 1993 to November 2011. — [Search summary](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=968338) (secondhand)
- **[UNVERIFIED – background knowledge]** The rule is defined by a cumulative-return window of J periods, a stop threshold γ (exit when the cumulative J-period return falls below −γ), and a re-entry threshold δ (re-enter when the cumulative return recovers above δ). The paper also defines a "stopping ratio": the stopping premium divided by the probability of being stopped out, i.e., the value added per unit of time spent stopped out. The "50–100 bp per month" figure is a per-stop-out-period measure, **not** an unconditional annual uplift.

### Inferences
- Because the 50–100 bp/month applies only to the months spent stopped out, the unconditional (whole-sample) gain is much smaller: the premium scaled by the fraction of time stopped out. This is an inference from the "during stop-out periods" wording.
- Proposition logic: under a random walk with positive equity risk premium, any time out of equities gives up (μ − r_f), so the stopping premium is negative. Under mean reversion (negative autocorrelation), stops sell at local lows just before rebounds, so it is more negative. Under momentum or persistent bear regimes, it can be positive. Stops still cut variance in most specifications, so risk-adjusted value can survive even when return value does not.

### Gaps
- The exact γ/δ/J values for the best-performing rules, Sharpe ratios, drawdown figures, and the fraction of time stopped out could not be pulled from tables (MIT/SSRN full text blocked).
- I could not confirm whether the published 2014 version keeps the exact "50–100 bp" wording from the working paper. One search summary flagged this as unverified for the journal version.

## 2. Lo & Remorov (2017), "Stop-loss strategies with serial correlation, regime switching, and transaction costs"

### Takeaway
On a large sample of individual US stocks (1964–2014), **tight stop-losses significantly lower returns versus buy-and-hold**. The main reasons are trading costs and *negative* short-horizon autocorrelation in single stocks. Wide stops on 10-day intervals roughly **match** buy-and-hold on a risk-adjusted basis by trimming downside volatility. Stops can win only for stocks with high positive serial correlation, or for assets with persistent bull/bear regimes.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Lo, A.W. & Remorov, A., *Journal of Financial Markets* 34 (2017): 1–15. The SSRN working paper is dated November 2015. — [EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/107017); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316575542_Stop-loss_strategies_with_serial_correlation_regime_switching_and_transaction_costs)
- Abstract: "due mostly to high trading costs, tight stop-losses significantly lower returns compared to buy-and-hold"; "outperformance is possible for stocks with sufficiently high serial correlation in returns". — [Search snippet of abstract](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284)
- Regime switching: "wide stop-losses are attractive compared to buy-and-hold for assets that switch between bull and bear regimes", especially when volatility is low in bull markets and high in bear markets and regime switches are rare. — [Abstract via search](https://www.bohrium.com/en/paper-details/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transaction-costs/811025757466787840-8235)
- Risk: "certain strategies succeed at reducing downside risk, but not substantially." — [Abstract via search](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284)
- Execution delay: "the delayed stop-loss scheme [one day] works marginally better than immediate execution for most stop-loss settings", and noticeably better for tight stops, because returns partially reverse during the delay. This points to short-term mean reversion. — [Abstract via search](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284)
- Costs caveat (authors'): the cost estimates "ignore broker fees and impact of trading and may therefore be materially low for some traders". — [Abstract via search](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284)
- Secondary (CXO Advisory summary): sample is US stocks 1964–2014. Measurement intervals are daily and 10-day, with stop ranges 0% to −6% (daily) and 0% to −14% (10-day). Trading cost is set at 0.2%, the average half bid-ask spread of sampled stocks in 2013–2014. Wide 10-day stops matched buy-and-hold risk-adjusted performance through downside-volatility reduction. Tight daily stops underperformed because of high trading costs and negative stock-return autocorrelation. — [CXO Advisory, "Stop-losses on Stock Positions in Depth"](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-premium/stop-losses-on-stock-positions-in-depth/) (secondhand)

### Inferences
- Stop width matters a lot. The tighter the stop, the more whipsaw (repeated exit and re-entry on noise), the higher the cumulative spread cost, and the more often it sells into short-term reversal. Width helps only up to the point where the stop still cuts tail risk.
- Single US stocks show slightly negative short-horizon autocorrelation (the bid-ask bounce and short-term reversal effect). That is exactly the environment Kaminski–Lo predict will make stops destroy value.

### Gaps
- No exact share of stocks for which stops beat buy-and-hold, and no per-threshold return numbers, could be retrieved (full text blocked).

## 3. Han, Zhou & Zhu (2016), "Taming momentum crashes: A simple stop-loss strategy"

### Takeaway
A 10% stop on each stock in a monthly-rebalanced momentum portfolio, monitored daily against the month's starting price, sharply cuts worst-month losses: equal-weighted from **−49.79% to −11.36%**, value-weighted from **−64.97% to −23.28%**. Over Jan 1926 – Dec 2013 in the US, it **more than doubles** the Sharpe ratio. The results are gross of trading costs.

### Cited Findings
- Abstract: "At a stop-level of 10%, we find, with data from January 1926 to December 2013, that the maximum monthly losses of the equal- and value-weighted momentum strategies go down from −49.79% to −11.36% and from −64.97% to −23.28%, while the Sharpe ratios are more than doubled at the same time." — [Working paper PDF (CICF)](https://www.cicfconf.org/sites/default/files/paper_811.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272247075_Taming_Momentum_Crashes_A_Simple_Stop-Loss_Strategy)
- Mechanics: daily open and close prices determine stop triggers. A stock is sold if its daily opening or closing price drops 10% below the month's starting price. The authors stress that daily information matters. — [Working paper (search snippet)](https://www.cicfconf.org/sites/default/files/paper_811.pdf)
- 2014 draft: stop-loss momentum Sharpe **0.369** versus **0.165** for plain momentum (monthly). — [Search summary of 2014 draft](https://forum.valuepickr.com/uploads/short-url/pZjwmAYhGmHwiZhz9LPbZW5yR6r.pdf) (secondhand)
- A summary of the 2016 update gives, at a 15% threshold: gross monthly Sharpe **0.40** versus **0.17**, and average gross monthly return **1.93%** versus **0.99%**. — [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/stop-losses-to-avoid-stock-momentum-crashes/) (secondhand)
- A secondary summary reports gross Sharpe **0.50** and worst monthly loss **−15.4%** for an equal-weighted 10% rule. This differs from the abstract's −11.36%, possibly because the version or specification differs. — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/) (secondhand; conflicts with the abstract)
- Critiques: the results are gross of costs, stops add turnover, threshold choice may involve data snooping, and covering the short leg at a stop trigger may need extra capital not matched by closing a long position. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/); [Seeking Alpha / Dual Momentum (Antonacci)](https://dualmomentum.net/2015/06/13/momentum-and-stop-losses/) (secondary)
- A 2024–25 Strathclyde repository entry, "Taming momentum crashes", suggests a later formal publication. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/90217) (details not retrievable)

### Inferences
- This is the strongest pro-stop evidence in equities. It works because momentum is itself a bet on serial correlation, and the stop truncates the left tail from crash months (e.g., 1932, 2009 junk rallies), which hit the short leg hard. This fits Kaminski–Lo: stops help where returns have momentum or regime persistence.
- The return uplift is partly mechanical. Applied to the short leg, the stop caps losses on the stocks rallying hardest, and the published figures do not deduct the cost of daily monitoring and intramonth trading.

### Gaps
- No net-of-cost or international replication was found in this session. Final journal venue and year are unconfirmed.

## 4. Lei & Li (2009), Snorrason & Yusupov (2009), Clare, Seaton, Smith & Thomas (2013), and other studies

### Takeaway
On individual stocks and broad indices, the weight of evidence is that stop-losses **reduce risk more than they raise returns**. Lei & Li find no return benefit once possible future paths are considered. Clare et al. find stop-losses on the S&P 500 do not add value, while monthly trend filters do. Trailing stops tend to beat fixed stops (Snorrason & Yusupov, a student thesis). Research Affiliates (2025) finds stops improve skew and drawdown but rarely Sharpe. Stops look better on lottery-like stocks (Dai et al. 2023).

### Cited Findings
- **Lei & Li (2009)**, "The value of stop loss strategies," *Financial Services Review* 18(1), DOI 10.61190/fsr.v18i1.4933. Abstract: "these strategies neither reduce nor increase investors' losses relative to a buy-and-hold strategy once we extend security returns from past realizations to possible future paths… the value of stop loss strategies may come largely from risk reduction rather than return improvement." The study looks at individual common stocks. Authors per CXO: Adam Lei and Huihua Li (2008 working-paper date). — [FSR article page](https://openjournals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/article/view/4933); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/using-trailing-stop-losses-to-reduce-risk)
- **Snorrason & Yusupov (2009)**, Lund University master's thesis (not peer-reviewed), "Performance of stop-loss rules vs. buy-and-hold strategy". It tests traditional and trailing stops on OMX Stockholm 30 stocks with daily data, Jan 1998 – Apr 2009, over three-month holding periods. — [Lund student papers](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/search/publication/1474565); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/PERFORMANCE-OF-STOP-LOSS-RULES-VS.-BUY-AND-HOLD-Yusupov-Snorrason/a6e73e9d0f2c1a7f18adfd9d4cb06cde3bcdfe6e)
  - Secondary figures, which conflict across summaries: trailing beat traditional at every level above 10%. At the best level (20%), trailing beat traditional by **27.47%** over ~11 years. Another summary says 20% gave the highest average quarterly return (**1.71%**) and 15% the highest cumulative return (**73.91%**). A third says trailing stops of 15–20% beat buy-and-hold. Trading costs were reportedly ignored. — [quant-investing.com](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/advanced-trailing-stop-loss-techniques-for-all-investors); [MoneyWeek](https://moneyweek.com/trading/602978/take-a-bet-on-this-infrastructure-specialist) (secondhand, conflicting; the 1998–2009 window includes two severe bear markets, which favors stops)
- **Clare, Seaton, Smith & Thomas (2013)**, "Breaking into the blackbox: Trend following, stop losses and the frequency of trading – the case of the S&P 500," *Journal of Asset Management* 14(3): 182–194, DOI 10.1057/jam.2013.11. York WP 12/11, 2012. The authors conclude that popular stop-loss rules do **not** add value on the S&P 500 (sample around 1988–2011), and that "monthly end-of-month investment decision rules are superior to those which trade more frequently." — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/17842); [York discussion paper](https://www.york.ac.uk/media/economics/documents/discussionpapers/2012/1211.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/yor/yorken/12-11.html)
  - A related paper by the same group (attribution uncertain) reportedly finds trend filters raise real returns by about **0.6% p.a.**, cut volatility by about one third, and cut max drawdown from about **74% to 29%**, results "remarkably insensitive to the choice of trend filter". — [Search summary; ANU CAMA WP 24/2013](https://crawford.anu.edu.au/sites/default/files/2026-01/24_2013%20Clare_Seaton_Smith_Thomas.pdf) (secondhand; index/paper not confirmed)
- **Masturzo & Jeon, Research Affiliates (Oct 2025), "Stop the Losses!"** tests instrument-level stops on alternative risk premia and on a 71-instrument, 252-day time-series momentum (trend) strategy. Conclusion: stop losses "rarely enhance expected or risk-adjusted returns, they consistently improve skewness and drawdowns". They can help in crises by curbing behavioral overreaction and limiting catastrophic losses, but "would certainly underperform" in some conditions. — [Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/publications/articles/1099-stop-the-losses); [Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2025/10/23/stop-losses)
- **Dai, Marshall, Nguyen & Visaltanachoti (2023)**, "Lottery stocks and stop-loss rules," *Global Finance Journal* 56. Stop-loss rules raise returns for lottery-like stocks (frequent small losses, rare large gains) because they cut losses while keeping big jumps. Technical-rule sell signals behave like stops, and the benefits survive transaction costs. A related Dai thesis finds stop-loss rules generally earn lower mean returns than the benchmark but limit losses, especially in declining markets. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1044028322000503); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/glofin/v56y2023ics1044028322000503.html)
- **Commodities:** Fan & Zhang, "Commodity Premia and Risk Management," studies stop-losses on commodity factor premia. — [AUT ACFR PDF](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/815659/Fan-update-RiskMGT_Fan-and-Zhang.pdf) (findings not retrieved)

### Inferences
- The studies that favor stops (Han-Zhou-Zhu, Snorrason-Yusupov, Dai et al.) share either momentum/lottery-like return distributions or a bear-heavy sample. The studies that find no value (Lei-Li, Lo-Remorov, Clare et al.) cover broad single stocks or a broad index over long, mostly rising samples.

### Gaps
- Not located this session: Macrynikola/Zakamulin trailing-stop work, Erdestam (thesis), and Detko et al. (2019) on stop-losses in equity momentum. None could be verified, so no claims are made about them.
- Lei & Li's sample period, stop thresholds, and numeric results could not be retrieved.

## 5. Behavioral side: the disposition effect and how stop rules counter it

### Takeaway
Investors sell winners too early and ride losers too long (Shefrin & Statman 1985). In 10,000 discount-brokerage accounts, Odean (1998) shows this is costly: the winners investors sell keep outperforming the losers they hold. A pre-committed stop rule is a mechanical way to force realizing losses, which is part of why practitioners value stops even where the expected-return gain is nil.

### Cited Findings
- Shefrin, H. & Statman, M. (1985), "The Disposition to Sell Winners Too Early and Ride Losers Too Long: Theory and Evidence," *Journal of Finance* 40(3): 777–790, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1985.tb05002.x. It extends Kahneman–Tversky prospect theory to predict holding losers too long and selling winners too soon, and coined "disposition effect". — [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360011145247011968); [Odean paper citing it](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html)
- Odean, T. (1998), "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?" *Journal of Finance* 53(5). Trading records for 10,000 accounts at a large discount brokerage show investors prefer selling winners to realizing losses, except in December, when tax-motivated selling dominates. The effect persists after controlling for rebalancing, share price, taxes, and transaction costs. The winners investors sell go on to outperform the losers they keep in later months. — [Odean, Berkeley page](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html); [CFA Digest summary](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/-/media/documents/article/cfa-digest/1999/dig-v29-n2-469-pdf.pdf)
- **[UNVERIFIED – background knowledge]** Odean's headline numbers are Proportion of Gains Realized (PGR) about **0.148** versus Proportion of Losses Realized (PLR) about **0.098**, sample 1987–1993. Winners sold beat losers held by about **3.4 percentage points** in excess return over the following year (252 trading days). These were not confirmed by any source this session.
- Research Affiliates (2025) frames one benefit of stops as "curbing behavioral overreactions" in crises. — [Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2025/10/23/stop-losses)

### Inferences
- The behavioral case for stops is strongest for discretionary retail investors prone to the disposition effect. A stop forces the loss to be realized, which also harvests a tax loss in taxable accounts. This value is separate from, and can coexist with, a zero or negative stopping premium in the Kaminski–Lo sense. Note the tension: Odean's evidence is that held losers *underperform*, which is consistent with stock-level momentum at 6–12 month horizons. Meanwhile, Lo–Remorov's daily/10-day horizons show *negative* autocorrelation. Horizon is the key variable.

### Gaps
- No primary-source numbers on how much a stop rule reduces disposition-effect losses in real investor accounts were found.

## 6. Theory: random walk, whipsaw, gap risk, re-entry rules; meta-findings by asset class and regime

### Takeaway
Stops cannot add expected return under a random walk with a positive risk premium. They destroy value under mean reversion, through whipsaw and spread costs, especially with tight stops. They add value only with momentum or persistent regimes. Re-entry rules and wider or less frequent monitoring reduce whipsaw. Across studies, stops reliably improve drawdowns and skew more than Sharpe.

### Cited Findings
- Random walk: "the stop-loss policy will always reduce the portfolio's expected return" if prices follow a random walk. — [Kaminski & Lo abstract via IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp234-254.html)
- Momentum or regimes: value can be positive "in the presence of momentum" (Kaminski & Lo). Wide stops are attractive for bull/bear regime-switching assets with rare switches and high bear-market volatility (Lo & Remorov). — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hhs/sifrwp/0063.html); [EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284)
- Whipsaw and costs: tight stops lose mainly to trading costs and negative autocorrelation. A one-day execution delay helps because of partial reversal. — [EconBiz abstract](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284); [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-premium/stop-losses-on-stock-positions-in-depth/)
- Monitoring frequency: monthly end-of-month decision rules beat more frequently trading stop rules on the S&P 500. — [Clare et al., City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/17842)
- Risk versus return pattern across studies: Lei & Li ("risk reduction rather than return improvement"), Lo & Remorov ("reducing downside risk, but not substantially"), Research Affiliates 2025 ("rarely enhance expected or risk-adjusted returns… consistently improve skewness and drawdowns"). — [FSR](https://openjournals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/article/view/4933); [EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284); [Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/publications/articles/1099-stop-the-losses)

### Inferences
- **Gap risk** (inference, no study found that quantifies it): a stop order becomes a market order once triggered. On overnight or earnings gaps, the fill can be far below the stop level, so the realized loss can exceed the nominal stop. Han-Zhou-Zhu's use of daily *open* prices partially captures this.
- **Re-entry rules matter:** without re-entry (Kaminski–Lo's δ), a stopped-out investor misses the equity premium after the rebound. The "cost" of a stop is mostly the time out of the market plus round-trip costs.
- **Where stops tend to help (synthesis):** time-series or cross-sectional momentum portfolios, trending assets (futures, commodities), lottery-like stocks, regime-switching assets, and bear-heavy periods. **Where they tend to hurt:** broad diversified equity indices over long samples, individual large-cap stocks at short horizons (mean reversion and bid-ask bounce), tight thresholds, and high-cost implementations.

### Gaps
- No primary study quantifying gap-risk slippage for equity stop orders was located.
- No formal meta-analysis of stop-loss studies was found. The synthesis above is the researcher's own.
