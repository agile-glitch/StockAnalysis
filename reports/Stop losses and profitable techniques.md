# Wide stops limit drawdowns; low costs build wealth

For a retail investor with about $10,000, a stop-loss works as insurance against deep drawdowns and against the habit of riding losers. It does not add return. Theory proves that stops must lower expected return when prices follow a random walk. Fifty years of US single-stock data show tight stops "significantly lower returns". Ninety years of daily data on 25,997 US stocks show that trailing stops tighter than 10% lose even their risk benefit once costs are counted. The app's own 2003–2026 backtest on about 190 large US stocks matches the published findings closely. All 20 stop rules tested lowered annual return. Wide 20–30% trailing stops cost 1.4–2.6 points a year, cut the worst drawdown by 8–14 points and slightly raised the Sharpe ratio. Tight rules (10% trailing, a 3-ATR chandelier, a 50-day-average exit) cost 4.7–9.4 points a year. The practical rule follows from that. Use wide stops (20–30% below the highest close, checked at the close), pair them with a re-entry rule, and size each position so a normal stop-out costs 0.5–1% of the account ($50–100). Expect overnight gaps to double that loss sometimes. On the profit side, the evidence that survives costs favours cheap, diversified, low-turnover holdings: broad index funds, plus at most a blend of the few factor themes that replicate (value, momentum, quality/profitability, low risk). Expect those factors to deliver about half their published premiums. Staying invested and tax discipline matter more than timing. The evidence on what loses money is just as solid: day trading, short-dated and pre-earnings options, chasing the day's top movers, high turnover, holding leveraged daily-reset funds for days, and publicly sold technical-trading systems.

## Stops buy smaller drawdowns at the cost of return

The theory is short. Kaminski and Lo prove that "under the Random Walk Hypothesis, simple 0/1 stop-loss rules always decrease a strategy's expected return". A stop moves money out of the asset with the higher expected return. It can add value only when returns have momentum (losses tend to continue) or persistent bull/bear regimes ([Kaminski & Lo, *Journal of Financial Markets* 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp234-254.html)). Their empirical test used monthly US stock-market returns from 1950–2004, switching to long-term Treasuries when stopped out. Some rules **added 50–100 basis points per month, but only during the months spent stopped out**, and before trading costs ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=968338)). Two caveats limit that result. It is an index-to-bonds allocation rule, not a stop on a single stock. And the gain over the whole sample shrinks to that premium times the small fraction of time spent out of the market.

Individual stocks are where retail traders actually place stops, and there the evidence is negative. Lo and Remorov studied US stocks from 1964–2014 and found that "due mostly to high trading costs, **tight stop-losses significantly lower returns** compared to buy-and-hold". Single stocks show slightly *negative* short-horizon autocorrelation, so a tight stop tends to sell just before a bounce. Wide stops measured over 10-day intervals roughly **matched buy-and-hold on a risk-adjusted basis** by trimming downside volatility. They reduced downside risk, "but not substantially". Delaying execution by one day worked marginally better than selling immediately, because part of the drop reversed overnight ([Lo & Remorov 2017, via EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/stop-loss-strategies-with-serial-correlation-regime-switching-and-transactions-costs-andrew/10013011284)). Their cost assumption was a 0.2% half-spread, and the authors warn this "may be materially low for some traders" ([CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-premium/stop-losses-on-stock-positions-in-depth/)).

The largest test of trailing stops covers daily returns on **25,997 US common stocks from 1926 to 2016**, delisted firms included, at thresholds of 1%, 5%, 10% and 20%. Trailing stops lowered mean returns. They also lowered total and downside risk, "especially during declining market states". After transaction costs, **the downside-risk advantage disappears for thresholds tighter than 10%. Thresholds of 10–20% keep it** ([Dai, Marshall, Nguyen & Visaltanachoti, *International Review of Finance* 2021, via AUT](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/research/using-trailing-stop-loss-rules-to-reduce-risk)). Gross Sharpe ratios were mostly below buy-and-hold ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/trailing-stop-loss-effectiveness-for-stocks)). Other studies reach the same verdict. Lei and Li conclude that stop strategies "neither reduce nor increase investors' losses" once possible future paths are modelled, and that their value "may come largely from risk reduction rather than return improvement" ([*Financial Services Review* 2009](https://openjournals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/article/view/4933)). Clare, Seaton, Smith and Thomas find popular stop rules add no value on the S&P 500, and that end-of-month decision rules beat rules that trade more often ([*Journal of Asset Management* 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/17842)). Research Affiliates' 2025 review concludes that stops "rarely enhance expected or risk-adjusted returns, [but] they consistently improve skewness and drawdowns" ([Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/publications/articles/1099-stop-the-losses)).

Stops do help in a few places, and these are the places Kaminski and Lo's theory predicts. In momentum portfolios, which are themselves a bet on returns continuing, a 10% stop monitored daily against each month's starting price cut the worst monthly loss of equal-weighted momentum **from −49.79% to −11.36%** (value-weighted from −64.97% to −23.28%) over 1926–2013. It also more than doubled the Sharpe ratio, before costs ([Han, Zhou & Zhu working paper](https://www.cicfconf.org/sites/default/files/paper_811.pdf)). Alpha Architect's replication on the winners leg confirmed the drawdown benefit but found little return gain at a 10% stop: 1.27% a month versus 1.24% without one. It also warned that daily monitoring and extra trading erode the benefit ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2016/08/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)). In lottery-like stocks (frequent small losses, rare large gains), stops raise returns because they cut the losses while keeping the jumps, and the benefit survives transaction costs ([Dai et al., *Global Finance Journal* 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1044028322000503)). Lo and Remorov find wide stops attractive for assets that switch between calm bull markets and volatile bear markets. Studies that favour stops on ordinary stocks tend to use bear-heavy samples.

There is also a behavioural case, and it is separate from the return case. Investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long ([Shefrin & Statman 1985](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360011145247011968)). In 10,000 discount-brokerage accounts, the winners investors sold went on to outperform the losers they kept, and taxes, rebalancing and trading costs do not explain the pattern ([Odean 1998](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html)). A pre-committed exit rule forces losses to be realised. This sits alongside two horizon effects. Losers tend to keep underperforming over months, which is momentum and favours stops. Over days, single stocks tend to reverse, which defeats tight stops. The stop horizon has to match the horizon at which the strategy's edge works.

A note on evidence quality: the full texts of several of these papers could not be retrieved during research. Their figures come from abstracts and reputable summaries. The abstract-level results quoted above appeared consistently across independent sources.

## The app's 20-year test reproduces the published pattern

The app's own validation module (`stockanalysis/validation/stops.py`, run 2026-11-21 on Yahoo Finance adjusted prices) tested 20 exit-and-re-entry rules. The universe was 188 large US stocks split into training (73), held-out (57) and final-exam (58) groups, and each group was run over 2003–2014 (including 2008) and 2015–2026, giving six test sets. Each stock got an equal slice of money and started fully invested. A stopped-out slice earned the 3-month T-bill rate until its re-entry rule fired. Intraday stops filled at the stop price, or at the open if the stock gapped below it. Close-based rules sold at the next open. Each trade cost 10 basis points, and the test had no look-ahead. The no-stop benchmark returned **11.4–21.3% a year with worst drawdowns of −36% to −54%** and Sharpe ratios of 0.72–0.90.

The results fall into three tiers. **Wide trailing stops (20–30%) and the 200-day-average exit buy drawdown protection most cheaply**, at more than 5 points of shallower worst loss per point of annual return given up, and they raise the Sharpe ratio slightly. **Tight rules buy the same protection at twice the price.** The 10% trailing stop, the 3-ATR chandelier and the 50-day average cut drawdowns by 22–25 points, but they cost 7.5–9.4 points of return and lowered the Sharpe ratio, because they fired 2.7–8.5 times per stock per year on ordinary noise. **Stops measured from the entry price (fixed 8–25%, 2.5 ATR, 60-day time stop) barely matter either way.** A stock bought at the start and held rarely falls 8–25% below its purchase price and stays there, so these stops fired 0.07–0.33 times a year and changed returns by less than 1.1 points. Stop-outs also did not flag stocks that kept falling. **In 62–72% of cases the stock was higher 60 trading days later**, by 3.5% to 23.4% on average depending on the rule.

The headline "sets won on Sharpe" figures hide a regime effect. The wide-stop Sharpe gains came almost entirely from 2003–2014, the period that includes 2008. In that period the 20% trailing stop with 20-day-high re-entry beat buy-and-hold on Sharpe in all three stock groups (0.91, 0.89 and 0.83 versus 0.81, 0.76 and 0.73) and cut worst drawdowns from about −52–54% to about −41–43%. The 200-day exit was more dramatic: drawdowns of −23% to −28% instead of −52% to −54%, and Sharpe up to 1.09. In the 2015–2026 bull market, both rules won on Sharpe in only one of three groups. The 200-day exit roughly halved returns on the held-out stocks (4.67% versus 11.37% a year). That is the regime dependence the literature predicts: stops pay for themselves in long, volatile bear markets and cost money otherwise. Nobody knows in advance which kind of decade is coming.

Re-entry mattered as much as the exit. With 20% and 30% trailing stops, re-buying on a new 20-day high doubled to tripled the drawdown improvement of re-buying on the calendar (+13.5 versus +6.2 points, and +8.2 versus +2.6) and gave the best Sharpe gains. The cost was slightly more return. The app's earlier round of validation pointed the same way: removing the trailing stop from the app's active trading rules raised training-period returns but did not hold up out of sample. Stop choice is fragile and easy to overfit.

Four limits apply. All the stocks are large and still listed today, so survivorship bias flatters every long-only result equally and leaves out the delisted collapses where a stop would have saved the most. Small caps, momentum portfolios and lottery-like stocks, the places where the literature finds stops useful, were not tested. Taxes were not modelled, and every stop-out in a taxable account realises a gain or a loss. Trading costs were set at 10 basis points, half Lo and Remorov's assumption, so the cost of tight stops is if anything understated. Finally, because every slice started fully invested at the beginning of each period, the fixed-stop tests do not reproduce how O'Neil-style traders use a 7–8% stop, on a steady stream of fresh breakout entries.

## Stop width and order type matter more than the method's name

### Each popular method has a practitioner default and little independent testing

William O'Neil's rule is to sell "any stock that I buy that declines 7% or 8% below my actual purchase price… always without exception" ([Motley Fool interview](https://www.fool.com/specials/2002/06/20/foolcom-tmf-interview-bill-oneil-special.aspx)). Investor's Business Daily pairs it with a 20–25% profit target ([IBD via inkl](https://inkl.com/news/when-to-sell-stocks)). The 7–8% is measured from cost and assumes a buy at a breakout point. It does not apply to a pullback from a high after a gain ([AAII](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/sell-rules-to-build-your-stock-investing-performance)). The research found **no independent published backtest of the 7–8% rule**. Chuck LeBeau's Chandelier exit sets the long stop at the **22-day high minus 3 × ATR(22)** ([StockCharts](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/chandelier-exit)). LeBeau's original anchored the stop to the highest high since entry, and the fixed 22-bar window is a later charting adaptation ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/chandelier-stop.md)). The Turtle rules use N, the 20-day ATR. The initial stop sits 2N from entry, each unit risks about 1% of equity, and exits come on 10- or 20-day opposite extremes. Those rules were designed for diversified futures portfolios, not single stocks ([IG](https://www.ig.com/en/trading-opportunities/turtle-trading--what-is-it-and-what-are-the-rules--180904)). No peer-reviewed test of the Chandelier exit on US stocks was found. In the app's test it was the worst rule tested.

Volatility-scaled stops make sense in principle even though no published head-to-head test compares them with fixed stops. For a stock whose ATR is 2% of its price, a 2–3 ATR stop sits 4–6% below the reference price, close to O'Neil's figure. For a volatile stock with an ATR of 4–5%, the same rule sits 8–15% away, so **a fixed 7% stop on a volatile name sits inside its normal daily noise**. On moving-average exits, the 10-month rule checks price against the average only at month-end ([AAII](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/missing-the-markets-worst-and-best-months)). Alpha Architect found that on the S&P 500, "the simple TF [trend-following] rule itself is the preferred stop-loss technique" over 3–15% trailing stops added on top ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/trend-following-the-sp-500-some-practical-advice/)). Faber's work shows why trend filters are a mixed blessing: **60–80% of both the best and the worst days occurred when the market was already below its 200-day average** ([Hedge Fund Alpha summary](https://hedgefundalpha.com/?p=375814)). No published tests of time stops or support-level stops were found. In the app's test, a "still losing after 60 days" rule cost about a third of a point a year and changed nothing else.

The evidence favours trailing over fixed for one plain reason. A trailing stop protects gains, while a fixed stop below the original entry stops mattering once the stock has risen. The evidence on width is the clearest finding of all. Below 10% on large single stocks, costs and whipsaw dominate. At 20–30%, a stop gives meaningful crash protection at the lowest price.

### The stop price is not the fill price

A stop-market order becomes a market order when triggered and fills "at the best available prevailing price—even if the price only exists for a few seconds or minutes" ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/3469406-stop-order-drawbacks)). A stop-limit order guarantees the price but may never fill "if there is significant price movement in a short period of time" ([SEC/Investor.gov bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletin-stop-stop-limit-trailing-stop)). The two flash events show the cost. The SEC's preliminary report on May 6, 2010 flagged "automated stop-loss market orders" as a contributor, and some trades executed at placeholder stub quotes ([SEC Historical Society](https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/2010/2010_0518_PreliminaryPlunge.pdf)). On August 24, 2015, **the iShares Dow Jones U.S. ETF traded about 20% below its prior close while the S&P 500 was down about 5%** ([AAII](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/69-editors-note)), and retail stop orders "came in for significant blame" ([CFA Institute](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2016/02/22/did-investor-stop-orders-or-liquidity-disincentives-cause-aug-15-flash-crash/)). Stops also cluster just below round numbers, and prices that reach those clusters move "unusually rapid[ly]" for hours ([Osler, NY Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr150.html)).

Gaps matter more than flash crashes for an ordinary stock holder. Take a $50 stock with a stop at $46 that opens at $42 after bad earnings. The stop fills near $42, a **16% loss instead of 8%, or 2R instead of 1R**. An alert app can turn these facts into design choices. Lo and Remorov's finding that a one-day delay helps, together with the app's own use of next-open fills for close-based rules, supports evaluating stops at the daily close and acting the next session with a limit order. Resting stop-market orders on thinly traded ETFs, or near the opening auction, invite the August 2015 outcome. Practitioners offset stops by a fraction of an ATR from round numbers and obvious lows. That is plausible, but it has not been tested on equities.

## Risking 0.5–1% per trade keeps a $10,000 account alive

Position size, not stop placement, sets how much a stop-out costs. The standard formula is **shares = (account × risk fraction) ÷ (entry price − stop price)**. Results are counted in "R", the initial risk, and a system's expectancy is its average R-multiple ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/r-multiple-framework.md); [Tharp, via Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/396639530/A-Short-Lesson-on-R-and-R-multiple)). Prop firms that liquidate traders at a 5% drawdown typically allow 0.25–0.5% risk per trade ([Tradeify](https://tradeify.co/education/risk-of-ruin)).

The research team ran a Monte Carlo simulation to show why the conventional 0.5–1% matters: 20,000 paths of 250 independent trades, each loss exactly 1R, with compounding. At **1% risk or less, a 50% drawdown essentially never happened**, even for a system with zero edge. At 2% it happened 7–16% of the time for zero- or thin-edge systems. **At 3% it hit 33–51% of zero-edge systems, and at 5% it hit 79–91%.** Even a solid edge, a 40% win rate with 2R winners, suffered a 50% drawdown 59% of the time at 5% risk. Losing streaks are longer than intuition suggests. With a 40% win rate, the chance of at least eight straight losses in 100 trades is 49%, and the median longest streak is seven. At 2% risk, ten straight losses cost 18% of the account. At 5% they cost 40%. Gaps make real tails worse than these i.i.d. simulations.

The Kelly criterion gives the theoretical upper bound and shows why traders stay far below it. A 40% win rate with 2R winners implies a Kelly bet of 10% of capital per trade, and 55% with 1R implies 10% as well. Those figures are 5–15 times the conventional 1%, and they are very sensitive to errors in the estimated win rate. **Betting twice Kelly cuts growth to the risk-free rate** ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5mr5k8qj/qt5mr5k8qj.pdf)). Half Kelly keeps about 75% of the growth with half the volatility, from the standard growth formula α(2−α). Fractional Kelly is the field's usual way to trade growth for safety ([Busseti, Ryu & Boyd](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/papers/pdf/kelly.pdf)).

The table brings out a point that is easy to miss. **The wide stops the evidence favours, combined with 1% risk, imply small positions and therefore 15–25 holdings.** That is good diversification for a long-term portfolio. A more concentrated 10-stock portfolio with 20% stops runs about 2% risk per position. That is acceptable for a long-term holder making a few dozen decisions a year rather than 250, but it sits at the edge where the simulation shows drawdown risk rising. Tight stops do the opposite. A 3% stop at 1% risk produces a third-of-the-account position, which is why practitioners cap any single position at about 20–25% of equity. That cap is convention, not a tested result. Reward-to-risk sets the win rate a system needs: breakeven is 1 ÷ (1 + reward/risk), so a 2:1 system needs a 33% win rate, and IBD's 20–25% target against a 7–8% stop needs only about 25–27% before costs. Around earnings, the gap arithmetic says to budget 2R, either by halving size or by exiting before the report.

## Only a few broad factors survive costs, at about half their paper size

Academic stock-selection findings shrink once they become public. Across 97 published predictors, returns were **26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication** ([McLean & Pontiff, *Journal of Finance* 2016](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Hou, Xue and Zhang re-tested 452 anomalies with value weighting and NYSE breakpoints. **65% failed a t-statistic of 1.96 and 82% failed a multiple-testing hurdle of 2.78**, including 96% of "trading frictions" anomalies. Their verdict was that "the key word is microcaps" ([NBER w23394](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23394)). Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen counter that most factors do replicate. They cluster into 13 themes and work out of sample across 93 countries ([*Journal of Finance* 2023](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/95651880/theis_ingerslev_jensen_et_al_is_there_a_replication_crisis_in_finance_publishersversion.pdf)). The two camps agree on more than their headlines suggest. Broad themes (value, momentum, quality/profitability, low risk, investment) are real but smaller than first reported. Niche signals that depend on microcaps are not.

Costs filter out most of the remaining signals. Novy-Marx and Velikov find that most anomalies with **monthly turnover below 50%** keep significant net returns when designed to save on trading costs, while few higher-turnover ones do. The most effective fix is a buy/hold band: keep a stock until it falls well out of the buy zone ([NBER w20721](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20721)). That rule rules out most event-driven retail ideas. Post-earnings drift, the classic information anomaly, reportedly faded from non-microcap stocks after 2001 and was essentially zero for large caps by 2006 ([Truthsayer summary of Martineau 2022](https://www.truthsayer.com/insights/post-earnings-announcement-drift)).

|---|---|---|---|

| Event signals (earnings drift, reversals) | Faded in large caps; high turnover | — | Costs |

Each theme comes with a specific pain. Momentum crashed when the market rebounded in March–May 2009. The market rose 29%, but the loser decile, which momentum shorts, **rose 156%** ([Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)). Value lost about **55% from 2007 to 2020**, its worst drawdown since 1963. The widening valuation gap between growth and value explains all of it "with room to spare", which points to repricing rather than collapsing value-company fundamentals ([Arnott, Harvey, Kalesnik & Linnainmaa](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P149_Reports_of_values.pdf)). The Fama-French value factor has still not regained its December 2006 peak, while the profitability factor made a new high in October 2023 ([finobservatory](https://finobservatory.org/factors)). Quality-minus-junk holds up across 24 countries, and quality stocks tend to benefit in flights to quality rather than crash ([Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-018-9470-2)). Betting against beta earned 0.56% a month (t = 4.02) in its original test ([Frazzini & Pedersen, NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16601/w16601.pdf)). Size is weak on its own and reappears only after controlling for junk ([Asness et al.](https://jacobslevycenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/Size-Matters-if-You-Control-Your-Junk.pdf)).

The live record shows how much of the paper premium a retail investor actually collects. Single-factor iShares ETFs charge 0.15% a year versus 0.03% for an S&P 500 fund. Over roughly the last decade, momentum about matched or slightly beat the S&P 500, quality trailed by about a point, and minimum-volatility trailed by about five ([portfolioslab](https://portfolioslab.com/tools/stock-comparison/QUAL/IVV); [portfolioslab](https://portfolioslab.com/tools/stock-comparison/USMV/QUAL); [pearler](https://pearler.com/explore/invest/us-shares/compare/MTUM-vs-IVV)). Those figures mix end dates, but the pattern is clear. Matching the index is already a high bar. **79% of active large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025** ([S&P DJI SPIVA](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/us-persistence-scorecard/)), and secondary tallies of SPIVA put long-horizon underperformance at about 90% over 15 years and 93% over 20 ([Evidence Investor](https://www.evidenceinvestor.com/post/has-your-fund-family-outperformed)). For the app, this means stock screens should rank liquid, larger stocks on combinations of momentum, profitability and value, rebalance slowly with buy/hold bands, avoid microcaps, and present expected excess returns as roughly half the published figures before taxes.

## Cheap diversification and staying invested beat clever timing

Trend following has a long and durable record, but as a diversifier rather than a way to beat equities. Across 67 futures markets from 1880 to 2013, a diversified trend strategy earned **14.9% a year gross and 11.2% after an assumed 2-and-20 fee, with 9.7% volatility and a net Sharpe ratio of 0.77**. It had near-zero correlation with stocks and bonds ([AQR white paper via TrendFollowing.com](https://www.TrendFollowing.com/whitepaper/Century_Evidence_Trend_Following.pdf)) and made money in 8 of the 10 largest 60/40 drawdowns ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/a-century-of-evidence-on-trend-following-investing)). The live record shows why investors quit it. After a lacklustre 2010s, the SG Trend Index returned **+27.3% in 2022 while a US 60/40 portfolio lost about 16%** ([Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/flat-december-sees-ctas-complete-record-year-2022/)). Faber's 10-month moving-average rule for whole asset classes cut the maximum drawdown from −46.0% to −9.56% in an independent 1973–2011 replication ([Juniata College](https://admissions.juniata.edu/offices/juniata-voices/media/andrew-and-castro.pdf)). Critics note the original test was essentially in-sample, and drawdowns grew once later data was added ([ThinkAdvisor](https://thinkadvisor.com/2015/09/02/most-downloaded-ssrn-paper-ever-says-you-should-be/?amp=1)). Put this next to the app's result, where a daily 200-day exit on single stocks cost 4.8 points a year. The moving-average rule with the best evidence is a monthly check on a whole index or asset class, not a daily trigger on individual stocks.

Volatility targeting is the main other risk-management tool, and it mostly reduces risk rather than adding return. On US equities, scaling exposure to a 10% volatility target raised the Sharpe ratio from **0.40 to 0.48–0.51** and cut the volatility of volatility from 4.6% to 1.8%. It had no meaningful effect for bonds, currencies or commodities ([Harvey et al., *Journal of Portfolio Management* 2018](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P135_The_impact_of.pdf)). Moreira and Muir's in-sample 4.9% alpha ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208)) largely disappears in real-time tests. Across 103 strategies, the managed version beat the unmanaged one 53 times and lost 50, a coin flip ([Cederburg et al., *Journal of Financial Economics* 2020](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)).

The techniques with the most reliable payoff are unglamorous. On rebalancing, Vanguard finds "there is no optimal frequency or threshold": monthly, quarterly and annual rebalancing give similar risk-adjusted results while costs rise with frequency ([Vanguard](https://indexacapital.com/bundles/unaiadvisor/docs/papers/2010-Vanguard-Best-practices-for-portfolio-rebalancing.pdf?v=241)). For a windfall already in hand, investing the lump sum beat a three-month cost-averaging plan **68% of the time**, and in all-equity portfolios it ended with about 2.2% more wealth. Cost averaging still beat holding cash 69% of the time ([Vanguard 2023](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/cost_averaging_invest_now_or_temporarily_hold_your_cash.pdf)). Tax-loss harvesting on the 500 largest stocks from 1926 to 2018 added **0.82% a year under the wash-sale constraint** (1.08% unconstrained), with more in volatile markets ([Chaudhuri, Burnham & Lo, *Financial Analysts Journal* 2020](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2020/empirical-evaluation-tax-loss-harvesting-alpha)). On a $10,000 account that is tens of dollars a year, and it defers tax rather than avoiding it. The wash-sale rule disallows a loss if substantially identical shares are bought within 30 days before or after the sale, and a purchase inside an IRA counts ([Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/wash-sales); [Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/irs-shuts-down-wash-sale-evasion-technique/)). That bears directly on the app: **a "re-buy next month" re-entry after a losing stop-out can fall inside the wash-sale window.**

Asset allocation and staying invested set the backdrop. Since 1926, 100% stocks has returned about 10.1–10.3% a year against 8.7–8.8% for 60/40. The worst calendar year was −43.1% for all stocks and −26.6% for 60/40, both in 1931 ([Visual Capitalist, Vanguard data](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/90-years-stock-and-bond-portfolio-performance/)). Bonds did not hedge in 2022, when US 60/40 fell 16.1% ([Vanguard](https://advisors.vanguard.com/insights/article/likethephoenixthe6040portfoliowillriseagain)). From 2005 to 2024, $10,000 in the S&P 500 grew to about **$71,750, but only $32,871 if the 10 best days were missed**. Seven of those 10 best days came within two weeks of the 10 worst ([Nasdaq/Motley Fool, J.P. Morgan data](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/market-timing-overrated-heres-why)). The statistic cuts both ways, since missing the worst days would help just as much. But because the extreme days cluster together, an investor cannot reliably avoid one set without the other. A rule-based trend filter makes that trade on purpose. A panicked sale makes it by accident.

## Day trading, short-dated options and chasing hot stocks reliably lose

Day trading has some of the strongest evidence in household finance. Complete Taiwan exchange data for 1992–2006 shows that **fewer than 1% of day traders predictably earn positive returns net of fees** ([Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, *Journal of Financial Markets* 2014](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt7k75v0qx/qt7k75v0qx.pdf?t=ohrd1z)). In Brazil, **97% of people who day traded index futures for at least 300 days lost money, and only 0.4% earned more than a bank teller's starting salary**. There was no evidence that they learned over time ([Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)). Across all Taiwanese individuals, trading cost 3.8 percentage points a year in aggregate, nearly all of it traced to aggressive marketable orders ([Barber et al., *Review of Financial Studies* 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:22:y:2009:i:2:p:609-632)). The US barrier just came down. The SEC approved FINRA's replacement of the pattern-day-trader framework in April 2026, removing the $25,000 minimum from June 4, 2026 ([FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10)). Small accounts can now day trade freely in an activity whose expected outcome is poor.

Options buying is the second large drain. US retail traders lost about **$1.14 billion on options from November 2019 to June 2021, plus about $4.13 billion in trading costs**. They favoured cheap, short-dated contracts with average bid-ask spreads of about 12.6% ([Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, *Journal of Finance* 2023](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2827/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202023%20-%20BRYZGALOVA%20-%20Retail%20Trading%20in%20Options%20and%20the%20Rise%20of%20the%20Big%20Three%20Wholesalers.pdf)). Option purchases ahead of earnings lost an average of **5–9%, and 10–14% for high-expected-volatility announcements**, because buyers overpaid for implied volatility and crossed wide spreads ([de Silva, Smith & So, Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/losing-optional-retail-option-trading-expected-announcement)). In same-day-expiry S&P 500 options, average retail losses rose to **$358,000 a day** after daily expirations began, mostly from transaction costs ([Bloomberg via BNN](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options-1.1910634)).

Attention and advice chasing lose money in a measurable way. Robinhood users' most-bought stocks each day, often the ones shown as "Top Movers", went on to earn **−4.7% abnormal returns over 20 days** ([Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz, *Journal of Finance* 2022](https://iimb.ac.in/sites/default/files/inline-files/attention-induced-trading.pdf)). That matters for an alert app, because the alerts it puts in front of users shape what they trade. The losses come from chasing what is salient, not from owning stocks: the aggregate Robinhood portfolio from 2018 to 2020 did not underperform standard benchmarks ([Welch, *Journal of Finance* 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27866)). Among stock-picking finfluencers, **56% are "antiskilled"**, and following them yields −2.3% a month. The less skilled ones attract more followers, and trading against them earned 1.2% a month out of sample ([Kakhbod et al., SFI](https://www.sfi.ch/en/publications/n-23-30-finfluencers)).

Turnover, leverage and chart systems complete the list. The most active fifth of US discount-broker households earned **11.4% a year against 17.9% for the market** in 1991–1996 ([Barber & Odean 2000](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/returns.html)), and men cut their returns by trading 0.94 points a year more than women did ([Barber & Odean 2001](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/gender/BoysWillBeBoys.pdf)). A rational adjustment-cost model can partly explain the pattern ([Bonaparte & Cooper, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16022.pdf)), but the direction holds across countries. Fund investors' buy and sell timing cost about **1.2 points a year** against the funds they owned over the ten years to 2025, and up to 2.1 points in the most volatile funds ([Morningstar *Mind the Gap* 2026](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/blt852a4301adc2f5db/6a7373d51f7b5ae2da78e1e8/US_Mind_the_Gap_2026.pdf)). Daily-reset leveraged and inverse ETFs decay in choppy markets. FINRA's example shows an inverse fund losing 1.82% while its index ends flat, and FINRA calls such funds typically unsuitable for retail investors holding longer than one session ([FINRA RN 09-31](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/09-31)). In crypto, more than **$19 billion of leveraged positions were liquidated on October 10–11, 2025**, across about 1.6 million traders ([Yahoo Finance/Bloomberg](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/record-19bn-crypto-crash-exposes-194741410.html)). With leverage, the exchange's liquidation price, not the trader, decides where the stop is. Technical analysis looks good in raw counts, with 56 of 95 studies positive ([Park & Irwin 2007](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jecsur:v:21:y:2007:i:4:p:786-826)). But once data snooping is corrected, publicly available indicators on US indices "do not have predictive power exceeding their costs", and no one could have picked the future best rules in advance ([Bajgrowicz & Scaillet, *Journal of Financial Economics* 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:473-491)).

## Rules for a $10,000 investor and the app's defaults

| Decision | Evidence-based default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| What a stop is for | Limiting drawdowns and forcing discipline; not a source of return | Kaminski & Lo; Lo & Remorov; Dai et al.; app test (all 20 rules lowered return) |
| Stop for long-term holdings | Trailing 20–30% below the highest close | App: lowest cost per drawdown point, Sharpe +0.04–0.06; Dai et al.: 10–20% keeps the downside benefit after costs |
| Stops to avoid on large single stocks | Trailing stops under 10–15%, 22-day/3-ATR chandelier, 50-day-average exits | App: −7.5 to −9.4 pts/yr, 2.7–8.5 stop-outs per stock per year |
| Re-entry | Alert on a new 20-day high after a trailing stop-out | App: double the drawdown cut versus calendar re-entry |
| When to check and act | At the daily close; act next session with a limit order | Lo & Remorov one-day-delay result; flash-crash and gap evidence |
| Initial stop on a new trade | At least 2.5–3 ATR away, never inside the stock's daily noise | ATR arithmetic; Turtle/Chandelier convention (untested for stocks) |
| Risk per position | 0.5–1% of the account ($50–100); up to about 2% only for wide-stop long-term holdings | Monte Carlo: 50% drawdowns appear at 2–3% risk |
| Event risk | Budget 2R around earnings; halve size or exit before the report | Gap arithmetic; pre-earnings option evidence |
| Order type | Prefer alerts and limit orders; no resting stop-market orders on thin ETFs or at the open | August 24, 2015; SEC bulletin |
| Taxes | Warn when a re-buy within 30 days would wash a loss; note that stop-outs realise gains | Wash-sale rule; app test did not model taxes |
| Stock selection | Multi-factor (momentum + profitability + value) on liquid stocks, slow rebalancing with buy/hold bands, no microcaps | McLean & Pontiff; Hou, Xue & Zhang; Novy-Marx & Velikov |
| Market-level risk | Monthly 10-month/200-day trend check on the index, not daily triggers on each stock | Faber; Clare et al.; app's daily 200-day cost |
| Core holding | Low-cost broad index fund as the base; active picks as a smaller satellite | SPIVA; Morningstar behaviour gap |
| Hard no's | Day trading, short-dated and pre-earnings options, top-mover chasing, leveraged ETFs held for days, paid chart systems | Barber et al.; Bryzgalova et al.; de Silva et al.; FINRA |

## Conclusion

The research changes the question a retail investor should ask about stops. "Which stop makes money?" has an answer, which is none of them on large US stocks. The better question is "how much return am I willing to pay for a shallower worst case, and which stop sells that protection most cheaply?" On that question the evidence is consistent. Wide trailing stops and slow trend filters cost about 0.2 points of annual return per point of drawdown avoided. Tight stops cost twice that and lower the Sharpe ratio. The protection pays off only in long bear markets like 2008. The stop's real value for a retail user is that it is a rule set in advance. It counters the disposition effect and keeps a single stock from wiping out the account, and that benefit does not show up in any return statistic.

The broader lesson is that retail outcomes depend far more on avoiding losing behaviour than on finding winners. The documented losses are large and certain: 3.8 points a year for active individuals, 1.2 points for fund-timing, 5–14% per pre-earnings option trade, and −4.7% for chasing top movers. The surviving factor premiums are modest and arrive at about half their published size. For the app, that means its most valuable feature is behavioural: alerts that slow users down, size positions, flag wash sales and discourage trading on whatever is moving that day. Three gaps are worth testing next with the same validation harness: stops on momentum portfolios, small caps and lottery-like stocks, where the literature expects them to help; an index-level monthly trend filter; and after-tax results.
